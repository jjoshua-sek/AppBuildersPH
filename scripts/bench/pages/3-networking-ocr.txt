2.1 How Data Moves

A network connects devices so they can share data. Every device needs an ad-
dress. An IP address identifies a device on a network, while a MAC address is
burned into the network card by its maker.

Data does not travel as one large file. It is split into small units called
packets. Each packet carries the source and destination address, so a router
can forward it toward its goal. A router connects different networks and
chooses the path; a switch connects devices inside the same local network.

Most traffic uses TCP, a protocol that guarantees delivery by numbering pack-
ets and resending any that are lost. UDP is faster but gives no guarantee, so
it suits video calls and online games, where a late packet is useless anyway.

People remember names, not numbers. DNS translates a name such as a school
website into the IP address that computers use. If DNS fails, websites seem
"down" even though the servers are running.

2.2 Protecting the Network

A firewall filters traffic using rules, allowing or blocking packets based on
address, port and protocol. Companies often place public servers in a DMZ, a
separate zone between the internet and the internal network, so an attack on
a web server does not reach internal systems directly.

Remote staff connect through a VPN, which creates an encrypted tunnel across
the public internet. Bandwidth is the amount of data a link can carry per sec-
ond, while latency is the delay before data arrives.
